Its fall within a few days has been confidently pre dicted at rebel headquarters, but at this writing the final attack has not come, although the attackers at some points are within range If they care to shell Spain's greatest city. The expected capture of the capi tal will of course make a prodigious difference in the nature of the war and in the status of the contending forces, but appraisal of the conse quences may be deferred. It has some times seemed that the directors of Loyalist strategy were slow to realize the gravity of the menace to Madrid and the urgent need of concentrating effort upon defense of the capital. Crucial Importance of Madrid In some wars possession of the seat of government Is not of prime import ance and its defense should not be allowed to hamper strategy unduly. But in the present case the Loyalist high command has revealed no strategic aims of such military im portance as to justify any impairment of the defense of the capital. And on the other hand retention of the capi tal has in this case a crucial import ance. By virtue of its possession the defenders of the Spanish republic have hitherto enjoyed an advantage rare In Spanish history. As a rule the Liberals in Spain have been rebels against a reactionary government. It might easily have happened so in the present case, for after the fall of the monarchy and the establishment of the republic a slightly different fall of the cards might well have led to a coup d'etat, the suppression of the constitution and a dictatorship such as has lately been set up in Greece. To resist such an overthrow of the re public its defenders would from the first have had to take the role of rebels, with no legal standing. Or it might have happened that the rebels were able to seize Madrid as they had plotted in the insurrection which broke out suddenly last July. In that event the elected government would not merely have been at great military disadvantage through the capture ot the government depart ments by the rebels, but the latter would have been able to set up a plausible emergency government at the nation's capital which might quickly have won recognition. Initial Advantage of Loyalists L In escaping at the outset both of these not improbable disasters the republic had an extraordinary stroke ot lu^k, by the standards ot Spanish history, and its leaders may not at first have fully realized that neither luck nor the will of the majority was i enough. The Leftist government elected last February had such pow erful enemies at home and abroad j that the legality of its status was a priceless asset' which should have I been paramount in the shaping of i policy. This need now looms up ominously as the trained rebel armies plow through improvised Loyalist and daily draw nearer to the gates of the city. It is generally expected that its fall will be the signal tor recogni tion of the dictatorship of Gen Fran co as the Spanish government by Portugal, Italy, Germany, most of Latin America and perhaps most other countries. It cannot be known, to be sure, how far the reactionary governments ot Europe would have gone in the way of intervention if Madrid had been more strongly defended and the rebel war machine had bogged down. Spain's Tradition of Military Revolt Recent events have amply justified the forebodings of the English jurist and statesman, Henry Sumner Maine, who in 1885 pointed out the disastrous effects in Spain and her colonies of the tradition of military Intervention in politics. That Alfonso had kept his throne for 10 years he ascribed to "statesmanship of a novel kind; as soon as he has assured himself that the army is in earnest he changes his ministers." Recent events bear out the conclu sion of this British historian that "The more popular the civil institu tions, the harder it is to keep the army from meddling with politics. Popular gevernments have repeatedly been overturned by the army and mob in combination; but on the whole the violent destruction of these gov ernments in their more extreme forms have been effected by the army, while in their more moderate shapes they have had the more for their principal assailant. Militarism and Democracy Of the antagonism between de mocracy and the military tradition Maine says: "Military insurrections are made by officers, but not before every soldier has discovered that the share ot power which belongs to him as a Unit in a regiment is more val uable than his fragment of power as a unit in a constituency. Military re volts are of universal occurrence; but far the larger number have oc curred in Spain and Spanish-speaking countries. There have been ingenious explanations of the phenomenon, but the manifest explanation is habit A army which has once interfered in politics is un^er a strong tempta tion to interfere again. It is a tar easier and far more effective way of causing an opinion to prevail than going to a ballot box, and far more profitable to the leaders." While Maine found Spain the out standing example of the military men ace to democracy, he adds: "There is probably no country except the United States in which the army could not control the government, if it were all of one mind and if it re tained its military material." This question of the political power of armies has become far more crucial than it seemed to most people half a century ago, and its grave interna tional importance has lately been shown by the domination of Japan by its powerful and aggressive military machine. Whiting's Boston Letter Boston, Oct. 28 --The moat interest ing event in town the past week was the production of "Hamlet" by Leslie Howard and his company. We attended the Wednesday matinee-- the thrifty price, of course--and the big Boston Opera house was packed to the roof. There were few vacant seats --in fact, an application was made tor seats on the morning of the performance and at that time the box office had nothing but a few seats in boxes. The outside agents had a few seats, but not many. We mention first this large attend ance, oecause we hear it said so often that Shakespeare "does not pay." It pays when it is well done by an actor of reputation. "Hamlet" is always a drawing play. We shall not undertake a Criticism of the play, beyond saying that we believe the production as a whole to compare more than favorably with any in the past. The setting was Impressive, grim and admirable. Mr Howard went back to the time of the action, in Denmark, and undertook to portray Elsinore as it might have looked. The setting was massive, dark, rather gloomy; maybe Mr Howard brought to the stage show something he has learned in the mo tion picture wqrld--the magic of scenery and atmosphere, the power of the picture. In this "Hamlet" pro duction the audience is overswept at the rising of the curtain with a sense of doom and tragedy; the light is dim, the backgrounds vague, the castle forbidding. Gloom settles closer and closer; and the soldiers look like soldiers, and Jike men seriously at tentive to the tougher sides of their trade. There is in all this production hardly a touch of lightness or of light. The doom and the tragedy mount and mount to the elimax. It is this, the general atmosphere of the production, which most appealed to us as being just as it should be-- and rarely is. As for Mr Howard: He is not the greatest of the long line of Hamlets, but he does not offend. If we undertook to be critical--and every actor who portrays the role knows he must meet a chorus of fault-findings--we might say that his Hamlet is a cultivated upperclass Englishman. If he had attended Har vard he would have been a member of the Porcellian; in Boston he would have been of the Somerset club. A very meticulous gentleman. Here was no rough, wild, raging Hamlet, no Hamlet thirsting for his uncle's blood, no Hamlet straining to avenge the death of his father. Rather, he was a delicate, timid young man. not a weakling, but certainly of no robustness; he was appalled, harried, terrified by his father's ghost--this, rather than stirred to rage and revenge. He was thrust, hurried, pushed, forced, into the role of avenger against all his inclinations. almost resisting the pressure, but at last going to the bloody climax in a gesture ot despair and horror, and no joy in the work. That is the impression that Leslie Howard's Hamlet made on us. And what of that? It is an entirely ten able assumption that the unhappy prince of Denmark was just that In deed, such a timid Hamlet is very easy to believe. Would not a more ardent, suspicious, eager, virile prince have suspected the treachery and the murder earlier than he did? Would he have waited tor the appearance of his father's spirit before getting any understanding that there was something rotten in his state ot Den mark? Filene, has lately lectured, or talked, to a group ot church dignitaries in New York, and among the things reported of his ad dress was an assertion that "th* whole theory of rugged individualism is visionary and impractical" To snatch a phrase from its context is unfair, and what are Mr Filene's ideas about individualism, and just what he understands it to be--or what anyone understands it to be-- we do not know. However, some ot the most vision ary things are worth pursuing; A reign of peace on earth and good will among men is visionary, to be sure; and in the present state of the world's chancellories and halls of state and alleyways of ambition and pools of intrigue, it is fairly to be called impractical. Yet we all pursue it --as a theory, a goal, a dream, which everyone professes to want, yet which if everyone wanted we would have. Very likely individualism, whether rugged or otherwise, was one of the first natural blessings or benefits or delights of the human race that it lost as it began to creep through the dark ways toward civilization. Yet, even as far as we have come away from that almost forgotten happiness--the right of every indi vidual to live his or her own life-- it is yet a vision which we can reach for and sometimes grasp tor a mo ment. What is it but the successful pur suit of the vision of individualism that sends a man fishing along the banks of a green-fringed stream? What else beckons him into the deep woods, where there is only for com panionship the songs of birds and the scent of flowers and the tang of old earth--and the comfortable com panionship of God? What else is it but the vision of individualism that keeps a man read ing far into the morning hours, by the fireside or by an open summer window, his eyes skimming thinly over a book, his soul running free and apart from the accumulated ter rors of existence? What but individualism leads a man to sit by the hour with a purr ing cat on his knee, his hands stroking the soft fur, and the silence of pleasant loneliness coming upon him like a shield and a protection against the elbowings and the hurts of competitive civilization? What is it but the yearning for individualism that makes business men dream ot some little farm far in the hills, where there is no comrade but the cows in the pasture, or the horse neighing by the bars, or the smell of wood smoke rising from the chimney, or the whirring ot a distant mowing machine? What is it but the love for be ing alone that sends men hither and thither across the world, professing to seek, but in their own hearts try ing to escape? Oh. indeed, the whole theory of rugged individualism may be vision ary and impractical: but it is th® secret yearning in every human be ing. It is the mighty inheritance of human kind: the right to be them selves --not the selfishness that dis regards the comfort and well-being of others, but that prays for some millennial society in which the massed efforts ot humanity will be directed not toward the prohibitions and sup pressions of one another, but towaid the expansion ot all. in the great blazing light of universal and eternal blessedness.
