Borah of Idaho symbolizes today The Man Who Waited Too Long. Perhaps the 70-year-old leader looks back across the years to a younger man who could arouse the masses, to the time when Borah was a name to conjure with, and to lost opportunities. Whether he really wanted the presidential nomination, or whether he was interested chiefly in liberal izing the Republican Party, his fail ure to rouse the rank and file in Ohio seems to demonstrate finally that the prize is not within his reach and that he can do little at Cleveland about party principles and policies. ADMITS HIS DEFEAT He admitted as much, himself, when he delivered an attack here on "the men who are in control of the party, who will likely be in control of the convention, and will write its platform and name its candidate." Those words carry the germ of revolt. They lend support to fore casts that the Senator will at last bolt his party. That seems to be a good bet. But no one ever has been able to predict Borah with great success. Once a bolt by the Senator might have meant something to him per sonally. But a bolt now would produce no earthquake. BORAH Ohio Democrats Poll Majority and Set Record Sen. Borah Salvages Only Five Delegates in Republican Fight Roosevelt Gets 489,000 Votes as Compared With 29,000 for Henry C. Breckinridge By United Press COLUMBUS, O.--A sweep ing victory for the "favorite son" slate of delegates over Sen. William E. Borah was in dicated today as almost com plete returns from the Ohio primaries gave the Democratic Party its first majority in a generation in this politically important state. Borah, veteran Idaho liberal, ap parently had salvaged five of Ohio's 52 delegates to the Republican Na tional Convention at Cleveland. His opponent, Robert A. Taft, son of the late President Taft, won the eight delegates-at-large and probably 39 district delegates. The Taft delegation will be vir tually unpledged since it will vote for its "favorite son" only on the first ballot. It had the backing of the state Republican organization. Borah, who made a determined cam paign, lost all seven of his delegates at-large with his nominees running an average of almost 80,000 behind when three-fourths of the precincts had reported. MORE THAN G. O. P. Democratic candidates, meanwhile, polled approximately 500,000 votes, nearly 100,000 more than the Repub licans with 7580 of 8579 precincts tabulated. The Democratic gains in a state that has been Republican for many years was emphasized in the vote given President Roosevelt against Col. Henry Breckinridge, anti-New Deal candidate, in the Democratic preference primary. With 8074 precincts counted the President had 489.631 votes com pared with 29,919 for Breckinridge. Roosevelt was unopposed for Demo cratic delegates since Breckinridge entered the preference primary merely to give New Deal opposition a chance to register.
