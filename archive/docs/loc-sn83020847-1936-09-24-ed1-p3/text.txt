•h National EDITORIAL DISCUSSION International STATE AND LOCAL TOPICS Gov Landon's Farm Program No one can read Gov Landon's Des Moines speech, last evening in connection with the prior announce ments of the President's plans for agriculture, without realizing the highly competitive nature of the political contest for the middle western farm states. Gone are the days when easterners could find a party that turned down the agrarian demands of the West for govern ment aid through some form of cash benefits, subsidies or bounties. The new deal's farm policies broke away sharply from the atti tude of the Coolidge and Hoover administrations, as developed, first, by the vetoes of those Presidents, second, by the futile Farm board of the Hoover period. With enormous crop surpluses overhanging the markets and depressing farm prices, the Farm board could buy grain without limit and still fail to stop the price deflation. For it had no means of controling new production. In desperation, finally. Chairman Legge toured the grain states ap pealing to the farmers to curtail their production voluntarily, only to be told in Kansas to mind his own business, or something to that ef fect. In its final report the Hoover Farm board, recognizing its own failure, recommended that it be granted power "to provide an ef fective system for regulating 'acreage or quantities sold or both." The triple A of the new deal had the power to offer sufficient induce ments, through cash benefits to farmers, for regulating acreage and crop production and thus reducing the depressive surpluses. The re sults have certainly had the effect of changing the farm program of the Republican party under its present leadership, regardless of controversy over how much credit should go to drouths and how much to monetary policy and how much to the triple A for the increase in the cash in come of agriculture from $4,377,000- 000 in 1932 to $6,900,000,000 in 1935. To state the improvement in another way, there was an increase of about 35 per cent in total farm purchasing power between 1932 and 1935 when the cash benefit system was for the first time in operation. ^he program was popular in the grain states until it was struck down by the 6-to-3 decision of the United States supreme court. Out of a total of 4,288,510 votes cast in six referenda by farmers on the question of continuing production control, in 1934 and 1935, no less than 3,707,642 voted for continua tion. Thus it became entirely beyond the range of realistic politics for the opposition to the new deal to revive the farm policies of the Cool idge-Hoover period if the great farm states of the Mississippi val ley were to be won back, or to per mit a farm program to be shaped by the doctrine of the supreme court decision that the regulation of agriculture is not a federal func tion. The Cleveland platform and now Gov Landon in his Des Moines speech accept the principle of na tional responsibility for agriculture and offer a program that seeks to compete with the Roosevelt admin istration's program in winning agrarian approval and support at the polls. An expert in farm economics is needed, perhaps, to expound the re fined differences between the com peting programs. Gov Landon of fers to farmers cash benefits, sub sidies, bounties--call them what you will--from the federal treasury to offset the "disadvantages" that agriculture labors under in the mar kets domestic and foreign. Like the Republican platform Gov Lan don at Des Moines seeks to segre gate "commercial farming" from the family type of farming. That this discrimination "takes the curse 'off" the cash benefit system for the regulation of agriculture is probably a matter for debate. How ever, the principle seems to have been already introduced in the soil conservation act of 1936, in suc cession to the blasted triple A, through a graduated scale of bene fits that discriminates against large scale farming under corporate ownership. The rivalry for the farm vote be comes amusing. Gov Landon insists that the President has no farm policy. The President's champions may retort that his land-use and conservation policy took shape as early as when he was governor of New York. Gov Landon takes a crack at the soil erosion and con servation program by recalling his own proposals two years ago and what has been done in Kansas. The department of agriculture, it is re plied, has been studying that sub ject for years and is well on the way. As for farm tenantry and crop insurance, the competition is sharp indeed. Before Gov Landon could deliver his speech at Des Moines indorsing crop insurance and setting up farm tenantry as a fed eral problem, the President had an nounced this week that he had formed commissions to formulate definite proposals for legislation concerning both. At a matter of fact, the Bankhead bill for reduc ing farm tenantry by a federal aid and purchase program was intro duced in the last Congress as a Democratic measure. How the agricultural West will react to this stern competition for its favor is a matter for conjecture. But Roosevelt seems to have the advantage of having already done something--not without success where his predecessors had relapsed into opposition or avoidance or futility. He has another advantage in that, if he is reelected, he will have a Congress in both branches under the control of his own party to carry on. Gov Landon, on the other hand, indirectly admitted, at Des Moines, the weakness of his competitive bid for support by an nouncing that in carrying out his farm proposals, "I shall call on the 'statesmanship of both parties." In measuring the future potential ities of the rival candidates in ac tual legislation, the western corn and wheat states can scarcely miss the inner significance of this con fession of Mr Landon's inability to give the country a responsible party government. [September 23] Our Dictators If the Republican charge of dic tatorship and subversion of the American form of government, which is one of the chief attacks on the Roosevelt administration, is not to be dismissed as merely similar to the charges periodically made by the party out of power against the party in power throughout the his tory of American politics, it should be compared with the attack which Theodore Roosevelt invited 30 years ago. Democracy is an ideal which must always be fought for; it is well to have the Republicans pledging them selves to it anew. But, if there is anything in Franklin Roosevelt's record or his psychology which so suggests the autocrat as Theodore Roosevelt's course in connection with the acquisition of the Panama canal zone and the protest against it, even from within his own party, as an act of "national dishonor," it would be helpful- to have some one point it out. Into all the circumstances of "the 'rape of Panama" it is not neces sary to go. Emboldened by the material success of the venture, Theodore Roosevelt became, in time, almost a sufficient witness without the need bf other testimony. It is a tragedy that the splendid achieve ments in tropical sanitation, and then in engineering, which the com pleted canal now represents, must always have as their prolog that sordid chapter of chicanery which led to the final act afterward de scribed by Theodore Roosevelt when he boastfully declared that he "took," and again that he "seized," the canal zone and left Congress to debate it. The violation of treaty obligations to Colombia, to which the United States government years later under another Republican ad ministration paid $25,000,000, was covered by the caustic comment of Atty-Gen Knox at a cabinet meet ing that he would advise against trying to give the performance "any taint of legality." It was in his reaction to criticism of the Panama performance that Theodore Roosevelt most notably succumbed to the temptation to abuse the powers of his office and furnished the most significant con trast to the course pursued by Franklin Roosevelt under criticism today. PROGRESS TOWARD SAFETY To the Editor of The Republican:-- I think you will be interested to know that 30 states' now have per manent state-wide traffic safety or ganizations. Some of these are safety councils, organized by private citi zens; others are departments in state highway or motor vehicle divisions of the state government. Literally hundred of our cities now' have functioning safety organizations. The Automobile Manufacturers' as sociation and a score of other na tional associations have worked close ly with the National Safety council-- making it possible for the council to coordinate, energize and support the national, state and local officials in the enforcement program. Let me emphasize that the press is playing an important part in the actual reduction in traffic deaths during the first seven months of the present year. Total deaths for that period were 2 per cent lower than for the same seven months of 1935, in spite of a 9 per cent increase in gas consumption. New York cut deaths 12 per cent and New Jersey 13 per cent--demonstrating that traffic can be controled in even the most populous, congested areas. Grade and high schools by the dozens have set up regular credit courses in safety and schools by the hundreds now offer automobile driver training to students. Harvard, Purdue and Northwestern universities are leaders in a group of institu tions of higher learning that have set up traffic safety departments. About 15 states have enacted and administered standard drivers' license laws with examination requirements and with revocation and suspension clauses. A dozen other states have weak license laws that need strength ening badly. From now on through 1937 the National Safety council will strive vigorously to obtain the en actment of standard license law's tor general adoption by all states needing such legislation. W. H. CAMERON, Managing Director, National Safety Council. Chicago, September 16, 1936.
