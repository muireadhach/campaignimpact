Governor Landon's earnest plea for an old-age pension and social security system that will WORK is one of the most stirring .and impressive utterances yet made in this campaign. The Governor's deep interest in the poor, the old, the unfortunate --the innocent victims of a world-wide eco nomic cataclysm--has never been better illustrated than in his Milwaukee address. But Governor Landon UNLIKE PRESIDENT ROOSEVELT--insists on a pension and security plan that WILL GIVE RESULTS. He demands that the money for jt be raised by a tax "earmarked for that purpose," instead of by borrowing gigantic sums which, as we all know, will be thrown away on boondoggling projects or similarly wasteful luxuries WITHOUT HELPING THE PEOPLE IT IS DESIGNED TO HELP. He pledges himself, if elected, to sponsor a pension and security law which will NOT be "cruel hoax" on the aged. Which will NOT penalize employment and exploit the employe. Which can be administered WITHOUT a horde of bureaucrats prying into the most personal details of the people's private lives. Which in short, will give security to those who need it and deserve it, without wrecking the financial struc ture of our Government and ending in inevitable dis aster for those who receive security and for those who must provide it. In the eloquent close of Milwaukee speech the Gover nor summed the entire pension and security problem up in two paragraphs. Read them and ponder on them. They are perhaps the first completely honest statement made yet in the campaign on this subject. As such, they ought to sink deeply into the consciousness of every American, young or old: "Let me repeat, I am a profound believer in the justice and necessity of old-age pensions. My criticism of the present act is not that its purpose is bad. It is that this act will involve a cruel dis appointment for those of our people least able to bear the shock of disappointment. "To these--our old people, our workers strug gling for better conditions, our infirm--l will not promise the moon. I promise only what I know can be performed: Economy, a living pension, and such security as can be provided by a generous people." Do you remember "September Morn"--the portrait of the young girl standing in a pool, in un adorned loveliness? One has to go back 25 years for the beginning of the tale. It began in Paris, as it has ended. There, in 1910, lived a prosper ous, affable, middle-aged and mod estly talented artist named Paul Chabas. For years he had ex hibited at the annual Paris Salon, attracting respectful notice, though very little enthusiasm. TWO ENTIRE SUMMERS In 1910 and 1911 he worked pa tiently on a picture which, though he could hardly have anticipated it, was to make him the most talked-of figure in the world of 1 art, and his work the storm-center of a controversy which arouses violent tempers, or equally violent amusement, wherever it comes up. Paul Chabas met an artists' model--a beautiful girl of 16, whose delicate charm fascinated him. Throughout the summers of 1910 and 1911 he worked on her portrait. To do it, they had to spend the summers at Lake Amency, near the Swiss border. Every warm morning, at 8:30, Chabas and his model would walk down to the edge of the water, where she would take off her clothes and adopt the pose with which the whole world has since become familiar. For 30 minutes he would paint, carefully and meticulously, for he was a slow and thorough worker. Then both would adjourn until the next favorable day.
