The Ohio, West Virginia and Ver mont selections of delegates to the Re publican National Convention Tuesday are favorable to the Kansas Governor for the presidential nomination. The Ohio delegates, elected on the Taft "favorite son" slate, 47 out of 52, are ready to vote for Landon, with few exceptions, after they have recorded a first vote for the son of the late President and Chief Justice. Half a dozen of them may be found voting for Col. Knox, Chicago publisher, but the rest are decidedly in clining to Landon. In West Virginia the bulk of the delegates elected are for Landon. The Republican State Conven tion in Vermont, without Instructing delegates for the Kansan, gave him hope for that State's delegation. Unless the Landon leaders are seri ously fooling themselves, the Kansas Governor will have nearly 400 delegates on the first ballot. It is likely he will have more than 400, from all appear ances. It takes only 501 delegates to nominate, with the entire delegate strength of the convention voting. Many of the delegates now counted for Lan don are "uninstructed," but known to be at present favorable to the Governor. It is possible, of course, that something might happen during the next three or four weeks to cause the Landon band wagon to falter--but it is not probable. The State-wide contest for delegates at large was a disappointment to the Senator, for Daniel E. Morgan, who was the high est man on the Borah delegate-at-large slate, was far behind the lowest man on the Taft slate. While hopping around in his usual calm fashion on the lawn, he omits from his song the variety of whirrs and duckings which he finds necessary while In the trees. These extra sounds In the lovely music of this finest of songsters scarcely add anything to the song, at least from the standpoint of the human ear. Perhaps they are all necessary from the viewpoint of the thrushes, but the listener will wish at times they were left out completely. The happy thing Is that they are omitted, while the bird is on the ground, searching for food. Then his song Is clear, pure music, slightly less loud than when uttered aloft. It may be that being among the branches of trees makes these birds feel more "pepfiy" and more in their element. It may be that when on the earth they are more pre-occupled with thoughts of food, and simply give the strains of their beautiful song as "music with meals."
If you want to hear the thrush song at its best, therefore, watch for the birds hopping around on the grass, beneath shrubs, especially rhododendrons, which they love. Then you will hear the real thrush music, which is about the bird world s best. Then, too, you can view easily the fine pink legs, which harmonize with the deep brown and soft grays of the feathers, and with the dark spots on the breast. Many think this is the most beautiful of all birds in appearance as well as in song. Surely there is some thing exquisite in the harmony of its colors and their blending, and the brightness of the eyes. The pink legs add just a little brightness to a bird almost as beautifully sedate in colora tion as Jenny Wren. This harmony is really appropriate ness. The wood thrush, or brown thrush, as it is known to some, blends with the earth, the greens of grasses and shrubs. It seems to belong, in a peculiarly good way. Bright is the car dinal, in bold red; sporty the bluejay, in blues and grays, but the wood thrush is really more beautiful than them all. Just as its song is. Every time the thrush gives part of its song, as it hops around on the ground, it turns its head sideways, and looks at you with its extremely bright eye. This is done calmly, without fear, though the bird may be only a few feet away. How the wood thrush, whose name shows its original habitat, came to be so unconcerned near human habi tation is a mystery. Its cousin, the her mit thrush, is as much a hermit as ever. In the early days of the United States the wood thrush lived up to its name in every respect, but during the past 50 years it has become the bird of gardens, in which it is as much at home as the owner. The wood thrush will hop along for ten feet or more, before uttering a part of its song. Then it will proceed leisurely for another few feet, when it will turn its head slightly, cock an appreciative eye. and sing again. Thus in a few turns around the garden it will run through its entire repertoire. This consists of four sections, or triolets, which may be turned into written music better than most bird songs. When aloft, sitting on a branch, giving its evening concert, the thrush makes various winding up" sounds, suggestive of an old-fashioned music box. These sounds somewhat resemble the discords which musicians sometimes put In their measures, so that harmony which arises out of them may seem all the more harmonious. The human musician who attempts to Improve on the wood thrush's song will have a job on his hands. It is easily put into three-four, or waltz time, but there the thing ends. Any attempt to build It up as written music must be built up results in something sophomoric, in com parison .vith the original. Thus Nature shows one that her efforts are not to be tampered with, except at probable loss.
The house wren must be both heard and seen for the true appreciation of its song. By itself, the wren song, even In the moments of courtship, is nothing much. To some listeners it is a cross between an unmusical whistle and the whinny of a horse. When the listener becomes observer, too, he sees the little fellow, a neat symphony of browns, fairly using his entire being in the song. Then, and then only, the song of the wren becomes something worth listen ing to, as the bird makes music from tip of claws to end of long beak, from his tilted pert tail to the very top of his round little head.
Last Sunday morning was brightened by a brief visit from the indigo bunting, a lovely and shy visitor to suburban gardens at this time. One will have to look sharp, however, if the bunting is seen at all. for it seldom lingers. It may be seen in grassy places where there is likelihood of many seeds. It eats sedately, remaining in one spot, and often not bothering to turn around. Look for this one, therefore, on the ground, a bit of blue there, slightly re sembling the paper used around absorb ent cotton in the box. It is an unusual blue, which again might be said to re semble slightly the reverse side of a sheet of purple carbon paper. It is entirely different from the blue of the bluebird or the blues of the bluejay. If you see this unusual blue on the ground--w'hich some namer long ago tried to designate by the word indigo-- feel pretty sure It is this bunting. He Is just a little larger than an English sparrow, but his color will make him seem larger. In attempting to talk about bird colors all observers must make allow ance for their own eyes. It is here, as with happenings--one observer will see it one way, the next another way, and seldom the twain shall meet. It is well, therefore, not to be too positive about colors as observed. The blue grosbeak, for instance, some what resembles the indigo bunting, but its wings have chestnut in them, instead of a soft wash of brown, as it is de scribed in the books. If two observers see the latter, one may see it "brown," the other "chestnut," without any real difference. Hence those not familiar with either, and with only colored illustrations to go by, will find it essential to read about the habits of the two, and note partic ularly that the bunting prefers to feed on the ground, amid grasses and the like. If their bird fulfills this require ment, it may be put down safely as the indigo bunting, whatever dispute there may be about its coloration. Colors, while important, are far from the only marks of identification. In bird observa tion discriminatory watching is essential. There has been a change of some sort, even aside from the expected effects of growing older. It is difficult, however, to tell just where the difference lies. The answer is provided by Dr. Ales Hrdlicka, curator of physical anthropol ogy of the Smithsonian Institution, from measurements of thousands of men and women--white, Indian. Eskimo and Negro. As one grows older, Dr. Hrdlicka says, the ears get larger, the nose gets longer and broader, and the mouth gets wider. The changes themselves are slight, but the total effect may be note worthy. They may be sufficient to throw the whole remembered pattern out of its former balance. Among the white "Old Americans," says Dr. Hrdlicka. "both the nasal breadth and length increase during adult life with age. but the increase in breadth somewhat exceeds that in length. With the ears conditions are reversed--they ii*crease more in their length. The Indians measured show similar conditions, and at least as pro nounced. The length of the nose and the breadth of the ears increase with age but slightly; the nasal breadth, however, and the ear length increase decidedly." This, he says, tends to increase me nasal index--the ratio between the nose dimensions which is a common anthro pological measure--very markedly with age, so that the differences between the oldest and the youngest approach those of distinct racial groups.
