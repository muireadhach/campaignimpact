Navy Bargaining. Norman H. Davis, able member of the American delegation to the Geneva Disarmament Conference, Is in London to confer with the British authorities on matters of mutual Anglo-American interest, particularly ill the field of naval armaments. When Geneva re cessed in July the conference had before it as unfinished business the Hoover propoeal for a flat all-around reduction of one-third in armaments of every category. It was agreed that pending the resumption of the Geneva discus sions In 1933 there should be Inter-1 power negotiations designed to pave the way to quicker decisions when the con ference swings back into session. Fundamental differences persist be tween the American and British view points on curtailment of naval arma ments. The Washington Government holds to the Hoover formula of β direct cut in gross tonnage up to thirty-three and a third per cent. The London govewiment proposes a reduc tion in the size and gun-caliber of bat tleships end of cruisers and smaller aircraft carriers. The British thesis was first projected at the abortive "Coolidge cruiser conference" at Geneva in 1927. It was re-advocated at the London Naval Conference in 1930. To it the one-time mistress of the seas resolutely adheres. On its face, the British proposal to whittle down the dimensions of permis sible warcraft would directly attain the primary objective of disarmament, as the United States itself has consistently advocated. But the United States' plea for disarmament, as a means of reliev ing the backs of overburdened taxpay ers, has always been premised on the theory that economy must not be sought at the cost of national safety. In his speech of acceptance on August 11 President Hoover, whose zeal for disarmament no one will challenge, called for such a defensive establish ment as would insure the security of the United States against Invasion by foreign soldiery. United States naval experts point out that thi3 country requires 35,000-ton battleships and 10,000-ton, 8-inch-gun cruisers because of the wide radius of action the larger fuel-carrying fa cilities of such ships permit to a navy possessing no far-flung bases of supply throughout the world. Great Britain can afford to operate with 25,000-ton battleships and 7,500-ton, 6-ineh-gun cruisers of shorter cruising radius, be cause, scattered over widely separated strategic areas, her navy possesses fuel bases, supply depots and repair docks. The American Navy, deprived of these inestimably valuable advantages, would be required to take the sea with "float ing fortresses.". That is why the United States needs bigger ships with higher calibered guns than the British declare they can get along with. These argu ments leave wholly out of account an other important factor in Britain's favor--her possession of a supreme merchant marine which overnight could be turned into a fleet of swift armed auxiliary cruisers. The case is succinctly put Dy ine Chicago Tribune in an editorial en titled "Navy Reduction at Uncle Sam's Expense." The Tribune says: "Great Britain possesses an unmatchable sys tem of bases flanking all the main lines of traffic. Her need is not the larger types capable of the longer sweep, but a preponderance of numbers. But bat tleships and large cruisers offset and endanger this preponderance. The British policy of reduction is to reduce and limit types efficacious in American 1 circumstances, while permitting the maintenance of a maximum in types efficient in British conditions. As this reduction is physically applied to both navies, it has the appearance of equal ity, but the appearance is fraudulent." It is not necessary to go th3 length of adopting the view that the British formula is "fraudulent." It is con ceived rather, it is fairer to believe, in that spirit of e;lf-interest which ever I characterizes British naval policy. Uncle Sam would pay John Bull the best compliment possible by emulating the theory underlying British policy and viewing naval reduction strictly from the standpoint of what Is best for the United States. A gardener must have a genuine taste for simplicity to appreciate these old standbys at their real worth. They lack the complexity of the rose, the striking features of the iris, the genuine grandeur of the peony and the sheer lovelinees of the gladiolus. Bt'.t they have simplicity. In this respect, of course, they are not unique. The petunia Is a simple flower. So is any one of the various types of daisies, including some of the single chrysanthemums. None of them, however, is simple in exactly the same way the morning glory is .simple. It has an utter lack of pretentiousness which is as refreshing in the flower world es the same sort of thing is among human beings. Among human beings this same lack often accompanies the moet genuine abilities. It is so with flowers. The morning glory, especially in the so-called common types, is a flower to grow where it may be seen every day, not necessarily to be hid in a corner, merely as a cover for some old fence poet. Early October days have brought these light blue and pink simplicities into their own. It seems to an on looker as if they have grown larger and larger day by day. something in the hopeful manner of their colora tions. which have become pinker and bluer as the days have overtaken each other. Morning glories, which open only In the morning, and night-blooming lilies to these flower lovers are far from per fect, owing to their stubbornness in remaining open at certain times of the day. There are other flower enthusiasts, however, who feel that this very trait adds something to their friends anchored in the earth. Such gardeners would not have all flowers bloom eter nally. It is well for the gardener, as for the garden, that all flowers do not bloom all the time. This is one of the Rood negatives of life and living.
