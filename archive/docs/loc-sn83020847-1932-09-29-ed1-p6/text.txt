Messrs Doherty and Ferry It is unnecessary to drag the pri mary system of nominations as an issue into the Doherty and Ferry cases which have been under inves tigation by the state ballot law com mission. They have nothing to do with the primary system. Neither Doherty nor Ferry was entered as a primary candidate. The same petty maneuvering for political advantage in the regular election can be practiced under the conven- Hofi system, because no convention system of nominations excludes the possibility of independent or minor party tickets and candidacies. The Doherty and Ferry candi dacies were based on the prohibition issue. Doherty was to run as a "pro 'hibition repeal" candidate for gov ernor with the design of drawing votes from Gov Ely; while Ferry was to run as a bone dry with the design of drawing the votes of Re publican drys from Youngman. Both mnn being obscure and without funds to spend, they were not ex pected by their sponsors to conduct a campaign for votes. The purpose of the enterprise would be fulfilled merely by having their names on the ballot accompanied by the printed designations, "prohibition repeal" and "prohibition" respectively, as a bait for voters whose interest in the state election might be confined to the prohibition issue. The exposure of the fraudulent nature of these two candidacies was easily brought about by proving to the ballot law commission that many of the signatures to the candi dates' petitions for a place on the official ballot were unauthorized and bogus. Aside from the discreditable meanness of the political tactics thus used by party politicians, the Doherty and Ferry cases are chiefly important as emphasizing the neces sity for the careful scrutiny and testing of signatures by the ballot law commission. Securing signatures has become too much of a racket by paid professionals in political cam paigns. The proposed preprimary convention plan cannot put a stop to the business. Jail terms can. That is what the Virginian called himself and the precedent thus estab lished has been followed in all later cases of a vice-president succeeding to a vacancy in the office of Presi dent. I am reminded ot the case of Fred eric T. Greenhalge. our governor who died in March, 1896, a few months after his inauguration. He states that this procedure lacks precedent, and The I Republican in its editorial comment j warns the wets of the complications that may arise out of this new and untried process of amending the con- I stitution of the United States. There is an element of Irony when such meticulous sensitiveness for a lack ot precedent is displayed at this time when the people are being of fered the opportunity directly to cor rect a national peril. This is an age of direct action by the people upon laws and upon men. Why preclude this direct and popular constitutional provision out of use until this day? Whereas the federal constitution has never been amended by the state convention procedure, there is a. strong precedent for the policy of this procedure embodied in the con stitution itself. The Articles of Con federation of the United States ot America, that the people of the states unsuccessfully attempted to live under from 17S1 tn 1788, evidently had a higher regard for state legislatures than had the makers of our consti tution. Under the articles of confed eration. amendment by state conven tions was not mentioned. Ratification had to be by state legislatures; any alteration in the articles required the I confirmation of the legislature of; every state. The makers of the constitution re laxed the rigidity ot this philosophy and specified three-quarters of the legislatures, and also offered popular ratification by three-quarters of state conventions. They very much recog nized the philosophy of the conven tion method, because they definitely specified that the constitution should be ratified by at least nine conven tions in the states to make possible the establishment of the constitution between the states so ratifying the same. Therefore, on a broad philosophy pertaining to the funda mental law they disregarded legis latures and stipulated conventions. If the proposed procedure is selected by the Congress of the United States there will be no basic breaking down of precedent. If Congress passes an amendment down to state legislatures for ratifica tion, by its action it leaves the matter indefinitely to the discretion of suc ceeding legislatures. With the 18th amendment Congress unconstitution ally tried to control the indefiniteness of the ratification by state legisla tures by putting a time limit on the amendment. Mr Dunford asks, "Who will call the state conventions?" It is quite clear in article 5 of the constitution that the Congress does not necessarily expect the state legislatures to do this. I understand that the delegates to the convention that framed the constitu tion referred it to the Congress and recommended that the state legis latures be advised to call the state conventions for the ratification pro ceedings. This, however, does not have to be done at this time If the Con gress believes that it is inadvisable and should consider another manner of setting up the conventions in the states to carry out the national will. From the statements made by Mr Dunford regarding the impeding pos- j sibilities of state legislatures it would j indeed be a risky procedure if tried. If it is maintained tha.t the state: legislatures are the only medium by which state conventions can be called then the state legislatures would i have the power to annul by their inaction the carrying out of the convention method as set forth in article 5. a power that they do not have, because the sovereign states with their sovereignty have ac cepted the federal constitution as the basic governmental authority of the nation. Article 5 manifestly and in herently gives the national Congress the constitutional power to call the state conventions into existence, be cause the source of the national power is the constitution of the i United States and the Congress is the legal medium through which it j works to carry out the national poli cies of its government and the neces sary details that go with it. Mr Dunford believes that especially 1 in these trying times there will be many states which will retuse to incur the expense of calling state conven- tions. He fur ther appeals to business men and wage earners to forget their imagin ary differences and act together to retain their freedom before it is too late. A very earnest plea indeed! The means to divert the present trend toward socialism and effectively retain our liberty is ostensibly con tained in the "Pittsburg plan," which is: The taxation rate, per SIOOO valua tion upon land to be double the sum levied upon buildings. Mr Codman claims that we tax building values heavier than we do land values and suggests that if this were reversed, prosperity would bloom and liberty would be safe. Does it make a difference to me when, for instance, I am to pay $75 taxes, $25 being for land and SSO for buildings, if the bill is changed to read SSO for land and $25 for build ings? LINCOLN AND HOOVER Parallel Found In Conditions Near the Close of Their First Terms To the Editor of The Republican:-- History shows that the situation which confronts the nation at the present time Is in some respects analogous to the political campaign preceding the reelection of President Lincoln. The Democratic party, then as now, was bitterly opposed to the administration. The war that was be ing waged to suppress the rebellion was termed a failure, and this, too, at a time when the great command ers Grant, Sherman, Sheridan, Thomas and others --were preparing to strike a crushing blow and lead the Union armies to victory and the nation to peace In the civil strife. Aspersion was rife and nothing seemed too harsh to say of Lincoln by his enemies, just as is being done with President Hoover in this presi dential campaign. Those Democratic leadens, however, were unable to mis lead the majority of the voters and President Lincoln was reelected. Times, of course, have changed and different issues are now before the country. But the same old tactics are being used now as in 1864, and there is little doubt that upon second se rious thought a majority of the voters this year will stand behind President Hoover as the men of the Civil war period stood behind Lincoln. It would seem as though every evil Influence was arrayed against the President--the bootlegger, the gang ster, the racketeer and the political charlatan. A victorious Democracy would mean a victory for these ele- ments. Imagine the condition of the country under such rule! It is generally admitted that the present depression is due to economic conditions. President Hoover is blamed for a situation over which he has had no more control than the humblest citizen. Notwithstanding this, he has approached the situation in a masterly way and has used govern mental machinery so effectively that the day of better things has dawned. And if the Improvement already be gun is not Impeded by foolishness on the part of the voter for a change, there is every reason to believe that normal conditions will again prevail in the near future. But the country cannot continue at the high-pressure speed which pre vailed during the years following the World war. A lower level must be reached both as to labor and the cost of living. Business and Industry are now upon a new basis, and on the ex tent to which the problem of em- ployment can be met will depend the future welfare of all the people. The present Republican administra tion Is working along the right lines. To place a Democratic administration, in power, having uo well-deflned policies and without the necessary ex perience in national and international matters, would be like sending a ship to at a without a rudder. WILLIAM H. PRICE. Washington, D. C„ Sept. 20, 1932. HAITIAN OCCUPATION Why the Legislature Opposes the New Treaty with the United States To the Editor of The Republican:--- The unanimous rejection by th« Legislature of Haiti of the new treaty which has been drawn up by the United States government is only one additional episode in a series that constitute a discreditable page in the history ot our foreign relations. The attitude, ot the Haitian people toward the new document is readily understandable. What was hailed by official Washington and by certain sections of the press as a. "Haltlaniza tlon" of military and other services in the republic of Haiti, really ex tends the iron financial domination exercised by the United States over that country, in the interest of Amer ican bankers. A loan imposed upon Haiti in 1922 over the protest, as the Nation ot the. 21st rightly observes, "of every articulate Haitian," carrying with it control of Haitian government affairs in the interest ot American holders of the loan, is now to be fortified by an extension of that control. The entire foreign indebtedness of Haiti, serving as the basis for this continued invasion ot a peaceful na tion, amounts to $14,150,000. European nations may default upon their debts. South American nations may cease entirely to pay interest upon their bonds. But the black republic of Haiti, which desires to refund the loan in its entirety, is obliged to submit to the United States dictation and dom ination, until every cent is paid. Furthermore, as the World-Tele gram has pointed out, the treaty which nominally provides for con tinuance of the American dictatorship until the repayment of the loan in 1942 leaves the door open to further extension of this tyranny in case there should be further funding. This is a travesty upon the pro testations of Mr Hoover that we would withdraw, as soon as possible, from our tyrannical and unjustined Haitian adventure. It is to be hoped that American public opinion will in sist that in this instance we come within measurable distance of prac ticing what we profess; and insist that American investors in foreign countries be denied the use of the United States armed forces and dip lomatic chicane in guaranteeing in vestments which are made at their own risk and theirs alone. American public opinion in partial reparation of the long and grievous wrongs done to Haiti, ought to Insist upon the prompt negotiation of a new treaty restoring full self-government to this Negro republic. HERBERT .1. SELIGMAN, Director of Publicity, National As sociation for the Advancement of Colored People. New York city, Sept. 21, 1932. THOUGHTS ON ECONOMY To the Editor of The Republican;--- An important question in connec tion with economy in government is that of the cost of distribution of our principal commodities. For example, the price of coal at the mines and ths cost to the consumer are at variance, also the price of oil at its source and the ultimate cost to the consumer, and the same with othe- products. The situation suggests consideration of government regulation, either by state supervision relative to prices, or national supervision. Economy of a constructive nature is not to be condemned, but when it is prompted by selfish or partisan motives and gratuities or other il legitimate revenue are not restricted or suppressed it becomes farcical and in equitable. Before the spirit of econ omy is made applicable to the salary and wage question, there is a service to be performed In correcting our so cial problems--the problems of graft and racketeering.
