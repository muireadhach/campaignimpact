Neutrality by Persuasion. The State Department's Instructions to American diplomatic and Consular officers in Spain, reminding them of this country's "well-established policy of non interference with internal affairs in other countries either in time of peace or in the event of civil strife" reveals what appears to be a curious discrepancy in the neutrality law. ¥-It is clear," says the memorandum, "that our neutrality law with respect to embargo of arms, ammunition and implements of war has no application in the present situation, since that applies only in the event of War between and among nations." Section 2 of the neutrality act provides that arms and ammunition and imple ments of war may only be shipped abroad under license of the Munitions Control Board, of which the Secretary of State is head. But it also requires that licenses Bhall be issued, except when the export of such arms and ammunition is un lawful--that is, when the President, under the neutrality act, has placed an embargo on their shipment. As the neutrality law plainly does not apply to civil warfare, the United States has no power to withhold arms shipment licenses or to prevent the shipment of arms or ammunition or airplanes--which have been classified as implements of war--to Spain, although that is exactly What France, through compacts With other European powers, is seeking to do now in respect to European-manufac miinifionc The only resort of the State Depart ment, correctly anxious to preserve American neutrality, is moral suasion, and this seems, so far, to have been suc cessful. Since the outbreak of the Span ish trouble no applications have been received for licenses and none has been granted for the shipment of munitions to Spain. It is reported that the State Department has, in reply to queries re garding airplane shipments to Spain, exhibited the memorandum of instruc tions to our embassy in Spain as indica tive of American policy. That, it is to be hoped, may be enough. The arms licensing section of the neu trality law, designed to prevent the United States from becoming involved In foreign quarrels through activities of munitions makers, was proposed in Con gress originally as separate legislation. Then came the demand for neutrality legislation, and the munitions-licensing section was dumped into the neutrality law. Failure to preserve neutrality in the Spanish strife is dangerous, possibly as dangerous under existing conditions as the same course in a war involving two nations. Yet the teeth of the munitions law are withdrawn and the law becomes inoperative in the Spanish conflict, with American munitions makers free to profit as they choose. Their apparent failure to take advantage of the situation show's their good sense, and the State Department is wisely emphasizing that patriotism is likewise involved. The Department of Agriculture in taking charge of settlement projects may 6ucceed in satisfying for a while the dream of the farmer who for many years has cherished the ambition to turn the Old home into a real estate subdivision. American taxpayers contemplate a Strictly neutral attitude in the light of knowledge gained by experience that war is an exceedingly poor investment. "The Black Legend." Americans who have not traveled In Spain and who do not enjoy intimate acquaintance with conditions there ought not to be uncharitable in their Judgment of the Spanish people in their present troubles. Neutrality certainly is an attitude to be desired on the part of all foreigners; it is the policy of the Government and should be that of the public in the United States. Particularly, fair-minded Americans Will wish to avoid what Spaniards call *the Black Legend"--that curious me lange of myths which suggests that a romantic but fearsome midnight con Itantly hangs over the Iberian Penin sula and that the inhabitants thereof are similarly wrapped in Stygian igno rance, superstition and wickedness. It happens that the composite picture painted by Victor Hugo, Blasco Ibanez and other fanciful novelist simply is not true. Civilization in Spain is no more notably tinctured with barbarism than civilization elsewhere. Neither the follies which Cervantes indicted nor the murderous tendencies which Prosper Merimee reported in his colorful fables are peculiar to the southwestern comer of Europe. The Spain, of "Carmen" does not exist, never has existed except in the imagination of its author--influ enced, as he was, by Borrow's impres aions and by his own natural penchant for exaggeration. Perhaps it would be helpful if an Obvious comparison were made. Suppose b the United States were summarized by Spaniards in the words: Gangsters, lynchings, graft, drought, Jazz bands, walkathon contests, automobile acci dents, the Townsend plan and the num bers racket. Would the resultant ver dict be accurate? Of course it would not! And the same observation may be made with regard to Spain as a land principally characterized by brigands, bull fights, guitars, mantillas, bomb throwers, castanets, incessant revolu tions, persistent beggars, universal thievery, professional Don Juans and that type of procrastination known by the name of "manana." Doubtless every country has its "black legend," but, if so, the fact is deplorable. A nation, it would seem, should be cele brated for its best aspects, not for its worst. Penalty of Fame. If any man ever deserved a prize, that man is John H. Hunter, who has won the Washington American Automobile Association's safe driving contest. In the thirty-two years he has been driving, Mr. Hunter is reputed to have covered 600,000 miles without any unpleasantness with the Police Department, without any blot of any kind on his driving escutcheon. That Is something for any man to be proud of, and if there were more John H. Hunters behind the steering wheels of our automobiles today this would be a far happier world. But while con gratulating Mr. Hunter, this is the occa sion also for the exercise of pardonable skepticism regarding his future as a safe driver. It is the time, in other words, to knock on wood. For by win ning the safe driving contest, Mr. Hunter has increased manifold the hazards which will surround him as a driver. One may envy him as the successful winner of a safety contest, but not his position now as the man who won a safety driving contest. From this time forward danger to that record will lurk in a thousand different forms. An ill natured policeman, a fender-scraping taxi driver, a horn-blowing idiot, a dreamy young man with a girl on his mind--these, one fears, are only a few of the forms that demons will take In their zest to deprive Mr. Hunter of his hard-won trophy. May he survive another 600.000 miles in health and happiness! But it might be safer to quit driving. A wood pewee was sitting on the stub end of a broken limb, waiting for in sects to come close. It was a characteristic pose and place for thijs bird, and readily helped identify him. Had he been on a full-leafed branch of the locust tree, he might have been mistaken for the phoebe, a bird of the same size and somewhat the same ap pearance. His selection of a perch, however, gave him away. Such small points are always valuable guides in bird identification. Many birds come to the suburban garden infrequent ly, fly around for a time, and then go back to the woods. The wood pewee and the wood thrush get the first part of their names from their usual habitat, but the latter is seen nowadays in suburban gardens more than in the forests, whereas the pewee only files in from time to time. It is necessary to keep a close look out for him, if the bird enthusiast is to see him when he arrives.
The great trouble in amateur bird identification comes in the gap between the bird on the bough and the colored illustration in the book. The enthusiast must realize that there is a great difference, not only between two-pictures in two books, but also be tween the same bircl in May and August. Feathers are constantly being shed and replaced by new. There is further complication in the fact that the juvenile birds are as large as their parents, and often look nothing like them, or at least are enough dif ferent in coloration to fool the beginner. Hence we find that birds may look differently at different seasons, and even month by month; that their babies may easily be mistaken for other birds, since they are so large; that there is a per plexing difference between the colors of male and female of the same species, in most cases, and that there may be a vast difference between two colored illus trations in two books. Let us not be harsh on the illustrators, but keep in mind the other three rea sons for differences, especially the first, that birds do vary, from time to time, and as seen by different persons. It will be realized that all other dis tinctions, such as whether the bird pre fers a dead limb or a live one, are val uable aids to the man or woman who makes no pretensions of being a scien tist, but who nevertheless has an honest admiration for the birds, and likes to know one from the other.
This is not an impossible tasK, in most instances. What is required, however, is a genuine interest. Without this, no one will be willing to keep an open eye, and it is the open eye, the seeing eye, above all, which is necessary in this work. Thousands of good people live sur rounded by birds which they do not know. "None are so blind as those who will not see." it has been said, and this ap plies here as elsewhere. "It is possible to live without pic tures." a wise man said, "but not as well." It is possible to live with scores of species of birds in the trees all around you, without once knowing them or car ing a whoop about them. At the same time it is equally possible to want to recognize them, to be inter ested In their life habits, to get a real "kick" out of their daily observation. Often the bird enthusiast will stop to realize that this occupatlop, which so Interests him, is utterly or practically unknown to most of his neighbors. They are satisfied with their automo biles, their radios, and so on. What are a few birds to them? And they are right--for them--until something or other turns their minds birdward, then they, in their turn, will stop row and then to wonder how it was that they did not know before how inter esting this pursuit could be. Especially the booklover should turn to birds, for there are many charming works about them, all illustrated with colored paintings of the most delightful nature. Even books on flowers and the like cannot vie, as far as colors go, with the books on birds. Then to correlate these pictures with the real birds in your own garden-- isn't that a happy occupation for mind and eye and heart, the great trio?
If you see a little nondescript bird perched on a dead branch, waiting eagerly, make up your mind that it may be the wood pewee. Then listen for its cry. If it says "pee-a-wee," as plain as anything, be sure of him. This cry is given with rising inflection, a question as surely as any bird can utter it. The first note is the highest, the sec ond considerably lower, and the last ris ing to mid-position. This is one of the plainest of bird cries which can be identified by the use of words. "Bob white" is perhaps the most fa mous. There are many others, such as "Sam Peabody," which one songster is supposed to say, and ''cheerily" and scores of other words which other birds have said, according to the interpreta tions of various ornithologists and just plain bird lovers. It will be understood that each one of these interpretations simply repre sents some human being's idea of what the bird in question "says." What the bird really says no one knows, or ever will know, but since humanity is very much agog at its own importance, of course, it follows that its members, in hearing the cries of other creatures, will do their utmost to wrest them to language as the world of men knows it. While this attempt is natural, and laudable, in a sense, since it is built solidly on the good rock of interest and admiration, it is wrong in another, since it means nothing at all, in so many cases, to any one else.
But this "pee-a-wee" interpretation is good, because it does, in many ways, give any reader a good idea of the cry. The notes are soft, rather shy in ex pression, with a wistful quality all their own. It is not repeated often, but only at rather long intervals, so the ears must be sharp to catch it. Once heard, however, and recognized as belonging to the little creature perched there on the dead bough, it takes on a meaning all its own, and is a precious possession thereafter. "Pee-a-wee-* Soft and gentle, a whisper of the woodlands, it floats in the open window in the late afternoon, one with the air, and the sun and the stars. There is a mysterious gas exuded from ripe apples which checks the growth of potatoes, protects stored potatoes from sprouting and rotting, and increases the amount of sugar in them five-fold. This gas. which cannot be obtained in amounts sufficient for positive identifi cation, acts as if it were ethylene. This is known to chemists as a colorless, in flammable gas with a suffocating odor, ordinarily derived from coal gas, which is best known from its properties as a surgical anesthetic. The amount exuded from ripe apples must be very small, but sufficient in a closed space to pro duce extraordinary effects. The experiments indicating Its pres ence in apples are reported by O. H. Elmer, Department of Agriculture chem ist, In the Journal of Agricultural Re search. That this fruit gives off a potent growth-inhibiting factor has been re ported before, with the implication that this was some sort of radiation. The present experiments show that It is almost certainly a gas. Duplicate ex periments with ethylene gave almost identical results. Potatoes exposed In a compartment with two cubic feet with ripe apples of any variety fail to sprout or send out oniy small, abnormal sprouts. When planted they send up not a single, healthy sprout, but many little ones. This, Dr. Elmer believes, is because the gas coming from the apples destroys the ''bud dominance" of the potato tuber so that all its parts acquire equal poten tialities in this direction. When growing potato plants are ex posed in the container, growth ceases, the leaves lose their green color, and leaves and stems are thrown into a sort of cataleptic rigidity. The plant acts as if it bad been anesthetized. The stem tends to become thicker at the base, but does not increase in height. The older leaves bend down as if they were wilted. About two hours are required before these effects start. Probably this period is required for the gas to attain sufficient concentration in .the atmosphere to affect the plants. Potatoes stored with apples for a few days taste much sweeter, and the reason for this is evident when chemical analy sis shows that the total sugar content has increased about 500 per cent. There is also a considerably greater output of carbon dioxide. This is a phenomenon comparable to that produced by expos ing green lemons and bananas to ethy lene, to hasten the ripening process. As soon as exposure ends, the effects on growth* seem to end immediately. Seed potatoes removed from the influ ence of apples sprout and the plants grow normally.
