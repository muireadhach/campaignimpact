Landon Disappoints by Playing Politics With Human Misery Governor deserves credit, says Clapper, for championing pro gressive ideas, but his good in appear meaningless tentions when he proposes to junk se curity program instead of offer ing promised plan to make it workable. By RAYMOND CLAPPER Gov Landon is in advance of his party in recognizing that the Federal Government must play a larger role in the affairs of its citizens than in the pre-depression past. Handicapped by a party which contains strong reactionary elements, his ef fort to champion progressive ideas entitles him to-much more zredit than his critics have been inclined to extend. It is all the more disappointing, therefore, that in his Mil waukee speech on social security, Gov. Landon, who promised suggestions to make the Federal act workable, actually pro poses to demolish it and substitute an old age dole for the needy. The Administration knows the present act is not perfect. Numerous amendments are awaiting introduction at the next Tinkering will be necessary for years. -ession of Congress. Instead of assisting in this perfecting process, Landon proposes to throw the basic features of the act out of the window. More regrettable. he makes a political football of the Gov enment's belated attempt to deal with a problem which has long received the attention of every other modern nation. In administering any program of social security, the Government must keep records. For Landon to denounce this necessary accessory to the task of staving off want among the unem ployed and the aged as a species of Federal bureaucratic snoop ing is to incite ignorant fears and prejudices. It is playing politics with human misery. Landon proposes repeal of the unemployment insurance title of the Security Act. This provision provides for a Federal pay roll tax, 90 per cent of which is to be rebated when em ployers contribute to state unemployment systems. Landon proposes to turn the whole matter back to the states, bo that they can experiment. This amounts to abolishing unemploy ment insurance. In the first place, states have not shown the least tendency to experiment in the absence of Federal inducements, such as are provided by the present act. Only Wisconsin and Ohio did anything on their own. In state after state, when un employment insurance legislation came up, business men ap peared and said the tax would be ruinous unless industry in other states were also put under similar taxation. They in sisted that unemployment insurance must be applied in all states to avoid penalizing industries in any one state. In the second place, the Federal act leaves to states full latitude to experiment. They can set up any type of insur ance they wish--and of 14 states which have acted, no two systems are the same. The Federal Government insists only upon certain administrative standards to prevent waste in overhead, and upon linking unemployment insurance with public employment offices to prevent cheating. In fact some of Landon's supporters denounce the plan, not for his reason that it prevents state laboratory experimentation, but because it does not require uniform systems. Great Britain has had unemployment insurance for 25 years. It has a long history in many other countries. If we are not ready to try it in America now, when will we be? Landon also proposes to junk old age insurance. In its place he would substitute an old age dole, so that every citizen over 65 would receive a "supplementary payment necessary to give a minimum income sufficient to protect him or her from want". He proposes to provide this fund thru a special tax, "visible and widely distributed." This can mean only one of two diametrically opposed taxes--either a sales tax which bears with undue weight upon the poor, or an income tax in which low exemptions would have to be practically abolished to bring everyone under it. It is important to know which method Landon has in mind. His strongest point of attack is against the heavy reserves These were forced contemplated under the insurance plan. Into the act against the advice of many experts by Treasury Secretary Morgenthau. They probably will be drastically cut down. However, few students of social security would abolish the thrift idea of insurance, whereby a person saves for his old age and draws payments based upon his earnings, and substi tute for this an old age dole. The act now provides for an old age dole to protect the seedy not covered by the insurance plan. It is a supplemental backstop. Landon would make it the whole program. The Landon plan is similar to that advocated at the Univer sity of Virginia recently by Winthrop W. Aldrich, chairman jf Chase National Bank. Both drew their basic material from Charles P. Taft III, a Landon adviser. When the present social security bill was pending in Congress, Taft joined in a telegram to Congress and the White House saying that while there was disagreement regarding suggested amendments, all signers agreed "on the broad principles of the economic security bill and the necessity for immediate action."
