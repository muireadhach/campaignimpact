HUTCHINSON ABOARD LANDON SPECIAL, EN ROUTE TO CHICAGO, Aug. 21 (I.N.S.)--Announcing he would accept President Roosevelt's invi tation to a drought relief confer ence September 1, Gov. Alfred M. Landon was speeding eastward to day through Nebraska and lowa to launch officially his presiden tial campaign in the industrial East. The Governor received a tu multuous welcome all through Colorado and Nebraska as he sped eastward from a brief vacation in the Colorado Rockies. At Omaha, where he remained overnight, huge crowds greeted him on his arrival, at his hotel, at a breakfast conference with Republican lead ers from lowa and Nebraska and again as he departed for his train. To the amazement of local lead ers, 2,500 persons attended the breakfast, where plates had been laid for only 800. He Speaks Briefly The Governor spoke briefly at the breakfast, emphasizing the need for public interest in local, State and Federal governments. The warm receptions enthused him to the point where he ex claimed "The harmony evidenced in our party in Colorado and Ne braska augurs well for our suc cess in November." To a crowd of 5.000 jamming the courthouse square, he added: "These large crowds evidence an intense interest this year in the issues raised in this cam paign. We Americans are re sponsible for the kind of gov ernment we get and this in terest indicates who are going to get good government." ANNOUNCES ACCEPTANCE Just before boarding the train at Omaha, he was informed of the text of the President's invi tation to attend a drought con ference at Des Moines, lowa. September 1. He immediately announced his acceptance. Pleading for a dispassionate de cision on the issues of the cam paign, Governor Landon appealed to the American people to take a I real interest in their local. State and Federal governments in order to be well governed. Speaking before the leaders from Nebraska and lowa at the breakfast conference, the Republi can nominee said: "We want government to do Its full share in protecting ns and helping us to meet our necessities. But we do not want government to jeopardize our birthright of freedom or mort gage that equality of opportu nity towards which we are striv ing. N. C., Aug. 21 (1.N.5.). --Martin Moore, giant negro, took the wit ness stand in hiS own defense to day at his trial for the murder of Helen Clevenger, New York University coed. Shortly after going on the stand Moore repudiated his alleged con fession to the slaying, asserting he made the statement only after being beaten and because he "was scairt." The State rested after J. F. Clev enger, the coed's father was called to the stand. He merely gave a physical description of his daugh ter and was then excused. As their first witness defense attorneys called Moore to the stand. Under examination Moore ad mitted owning the gun, which was shown by the State to have killed the coed, but he insisted he was not in possession of the revolver the night the girl was slain. Moore said he got the gun back later. "What did you do with it?" "I hid it." "Why?" "I was scared." Moore asserted he had given the gun to L. D.
